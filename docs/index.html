How it works

Five steps, built in order

Courtline follows the same rule as Gridline: build projections from public data, test them only on games the model has not seen, and trust only what holds up against the closing line.

  1. Collect the data

    Team and player box scores, schedules, and consensus closing spreads and totals for about 7,900 games from July 2020 to June 2026, from the open hoopR NBA dataset.

  2. Model team form

    Exponentially weighted offensive rating, defensive rating and pace with a 12 game half life, regressed 30% toward average between seasons, plus rest days and back to backs.

  3. Backtest walk forward

    Each season from 2021–22 to 2025–26 is projected only from the games before it, then scored against final results and the closing line.

  4. Save the market

    A scheduled daily pull from The Odds API saves spreads, totals and moneylines across US sportsbooks as timestamped snapshots, so every projection can be graded on closing line value. It runs on about 90 credits a month, inside the free tier.

  5. Paper trade first

    Every projection is logged with its line, lean and edge, then graded against results and the close for four to six weeks before any real money.

What the backtest found

The market already knew

The model agreed with the closing line so closely that it almost never moved a spread by a full point. On the games where it did lean one way, no group cleared the 52.4% win rate needed to profit at standard −110 pricing.

That is the expected answer. NBA closing lines are some of the most efficient prices in sports, and team form is already built into them. A backtest that reports this plainly is doing its job.

One thread to pull

Tired road teams and the total

One pattern stood out from the noise. When the road team was playing its second game in two nights, games finished over the closing total by more than a point on average. At about 2.3 standard errors it is suggestive and unproven, so it gets retested on new games before it counts for anything.

What comes next

Player minutes and props

If a model like this has an NBA edge anywhere, props are the likeliest place: thinner markets, many more lines, and slower adjustments to minutes and role changes. The plan is a player minutes model first, then props, then totals, with sides last if ever.

The obstacle is data. No public source has historical player prop lines, so Courtline will save its own prop odds going forward and grade early projections against results until enough market history builds up.
